Reference · What a custom column can compute

BattleGrid Column Algebra

Every custom column is one metric put through at most two operators. Sixteen operators exist, they fall into seven mathematical classes, and exactly one of those classes can be composed. But the column is only the first of four layers that compute — and the ones above it are where the cross-asset and logical families live.

Metrics 86 Operators 16 Legal atoms 322 Structural shapes 488 With operands & orderings 2,200 Fully parameterised ~70,700

Four layers, not one

It is easy to read “custom tables” as a single feature. It is a stack, and each level has its own algebra. What one level computes becomes an input to the next.

LayerOperates onAlgebraProduces
1 · Column one metric, one coin 16 operators, 2 stages, 5 parameter axes a number or a label
2 · Section a set of columns benchmarkTicker binds the section to a fixed ticker this coin’s reading, or a reference coin’s
3 · Condition headers, addressed by section thresholds + ALL ANY NOT N_OF, composed as a DAG TRUE / FALSE, and a verdict
4 · Scorecard platform signals, not columns weighted mean over signals that fired, allocations 0–3 as weights an aggregate score, gated

This page covers layers 1 to 3. Layer 4 consumes platform signals rather than the columns you author, so it is a separate subject.

The grammar

A column is a pipeline. You choose a metric, apply one operator to get a quantity, and optionally apply a second operator to turn that quantity into a statistic. The second stage is where the interesting indicators live, and it is the stage with the tightest rule.

Distance, spread and nearest-zone-distance are one operation. All three compute (a − b) / b × 100; they differ only in what b is — a price level, another metric, or a structure zone's midpoint. That is why they are the most productive class in the whole system despite being three entries in a list of sixteen.

The composition law

Only the normalised-gap class can be chained. Of the sixteen operators, exactly two — spread and distance — accept a second stage. Everything else is terminal.

Stage 2 offers four operators, and the chained shapes divide almost evenly between the first three: 

stage 1                 stage 2                 shapes
spread   (118 shapes) ──┐   trajectory            52
distance  (48 shapes) ──┤   aggregate             52
                        ├─  efficiency            52
                        └─  rank                  10   (distance always;
                                                        spread only where the
                                                        operand is whitelisted)

This is not an arbitrary restriction — it is the system telling you its opinion. The window operators already work directly on raw metrics (aggregate, efficiency, trajectory and rank are all available as stage 1). So chaining adds exactly one capability: applying a window or a peer rank to a quantity that has already been normalised into a percentage.

That matters because of units. The mean of EMA5 is a price — meaningless across coins. The mean of EMA5 spread EMA13 is a percentage — directly comparable between BTC and DOGE. Chaining is the mechanism for cross-coin-comparable second-order statistics, and the grammar refuses to let you build the meaningless version.

Parameters are a fifth axis, not a detail

Seven of the sixteen operators take parameters, and they are not cosmetic — they change what the operator computes. An efficiency at window 4 and one at window 32 are different indicators, not one indicator configured twice.

One budget governs the whole parameter axis. Every column has a lookback of window + offset, and the report’s largest lookback is capped at 32. That is what limits window to 32 rather than the schema’s 64 — and what limits offset to 8, because a plain value column carries an implicit window of 24.

Counting the parameter axes against that cap, the 2,200 shapes become roughly 70,700 distinct columns. spread accounts for most of it: 57 metrics, up to 17 operands each, four chain options, each with its own window and bars.

The fan-out matters too. A trajectory of window N emits N + 1 headers — _t(N−1) … _t1, _now, _trend. At window 32 that is 33 headers from a single column, which costs one of the 32 sectionColumns but consumes the report’s width and its token budget.

What you can build

Nine indicator families follow from the primitives above. Each is a real column spec, not a sketch.

Normalised stretch 
X spread Y        →   (X − Y) / Y × 100

Any metric against any whitelisted operand. 57 metrics offer spread, most with 14–17 operand choices. Price against a moving average, one MA against another, mark against spot, perp CVD against spot CVD.

Stretch history — is the gap opening or closing? 
X spread Y → trajectory   →   last N gap values + direction

The slots hold the spread series, not the base metric. This is the cleanest divergence primitive in the system: the gap's own trend, in one column.

Mean-reversion baseline 
X spread Y → aggregate   →   mean gap over the window (default 24)

Ship this beside the un-chained spread and a condition can compare current gap to its own recent mean — a reversion signal built from two columns and one clause.

Trend purity (Kaufman efficiency ratio) 
X → efficiency              →   |net move| / Σ|steps|,  window 21

1.0 is a straight line, near 0 is chop. Works on a raw metric or on a normalised gap. The closest thing the system has to a noise measure.

Cross-coin stretch ranking 
X distance → rank           →   which coin is most stretched, n/N

The only construct that leaves the coin and looks at the field. Four orderings — hi, lo, far, near.

Burstiness / concentration 
X → maxShare                →   max(x) / Σx over 24 observations

1.0 means one bar carried the entire window; 1/24 is perfectly even. A single-number answer to "was this move one candle or a sustained push?"

Regime and event labels 
X → classifyZone · bandTouch · crossDetect

Categorical reads. crossDetect is a discrete first derivative — the sign change between two consecutive bars. Note that ADX_zone and MFI14_zone are display-only and cannot carry a condition; use numeric thresholds instead.

Structure proximity 
STRUCT_ZONES → nearestZoneDist · Type · Range · Age   (side: support | resistance)

Distance to the nearest order block or fair-value gap, what kind it is, how wide, and how many hours old. Four columns describe a level completely.

Multi-timeframe versions of any of the above 
timeframe: { rel: "anchor" | "lower" | "regime" }
timeframe: { abs: "1m" … "1w" }        ← 13 fixed intervals

Two addressing modes, and the absolute one is the stronger: a column can be pinned to a fixed interval regardless of the section anchor, so 1h RSI and 1d RSI sit in one table without touching the section. Rank is the exception — it compiles as timeframeMode: "timeless", so the timeframe is accepted and ignored.

Layer 2 — the section binds a reference

A custom section carries benchmarkTicker. Setting it does not change what the columns compute; it changes whose data they read. The section renders one row for that ticker, identical no matter which coin is being evaluated: 

| coin | chg24h | RSI14 |     ← section bound to BTC
|------|--------|-------|
| BTC  | +1.46% |  59.0 |     the same row for every coin evaluated

That is the cross-asset primitive. Two sections with identical columns — one bound, one not — put this coin and the benchmark side by side in one report. Headers collide only within a section; across sections the platform qualifies them by section key, and a clause names the sectionKey it means.

You may supply your own sectionKey as custom:<uuid> — the server accepts it rather than minting one. That removes the compile-first step when authoring conditions, because you already know the key you will reference.

This is what makes relative-strength families buildable: coin up while benchmark down (decoupling), coin up with benchmark up (beta ride), coin oversold while the benchmark is not. Verified live from two threshold clauses and one group — TRUE on ETH and SOL, FALSE on DOGE.

Layer 3 — the condition algebra

Conditions are where constants live. A column takes no coefficient; a clause compares a header to a literal, and the connectives compose those predicates into a DAG.

N_OF is the one with no column-layer equivalent: a majority-style gate that counts how many members hold. “Two of these three agree” is not expressible in a column, and is one clause here.

The documented third state is not reachable from null data The platform says TRUE / FALSE / UNRESOLVED are three distinct states, and that UNRESOLVED means an input was missing. Measured, a null column value returns FALSE: crowdAccLive rendered — and crowdAccLive gt 50 read FALSE with unresolvedCount: 0. For null data the logic is two-valued, and “missing” is indistinguishable from “wrong”. Pair any clause on a nullable metric with a presence check.

What you cannot build

The boundary is as useful as the surface. These are absent from the operator set entirely — not hard, not undocumented, absent.

No dispersion — so no true z-score There is no stddev, variance, percentile or median. The only reducers are mean, max-share and efficiency. A z-score needs a standard deviation, so it cannot be computed in a column. efficiency is the nearest available proxy for "how noisy has this been", and it is not the same quantity.
No free arithmetic between two columns You cannot divide column A by column B of your choosing. The only metric-to-metric operation is spread, and only against operands the contract whitelists for that metric.
A lag cannot sit beside its own present offset reads a metric N bars back, but the header carries only the metric code — close at every offset. Two value columns on one metric collide and the section is dropped. Use trajectory, whose slots are distinct headers, or put them in separate sections.
No coefficients or constants A column takes no user-supplied multiplier or offset term. Thresholds live in the conditions layer, not in the column's arithmetic.
Two stages, and no chain onto a chain chainedTransformId is a single field. You cannot take a spread, average it, and then rank the average.
No conditional or piecewise arithmetic A column computes one expression unconditionally. Branching belongs to the conditions layer, which reads columns and returns TRUE / FALSE / UNRESOLVED.
No correlation or covariance as a number Nothing returns a ρ or a β. Cross-asset comparison is available — bind a section to a benchmark and compare thresholded reads — but the result is a Boolean relationship, not a coefficient.

A single report holds up to 32 columns across up to 32 sections, so any one strategy samples a very small part of this. Of the 488 structural shapes, platform templates use 139 — leaving 349 that no shipped template has ever put on screen.

Reading the table as a whole

If you want one sentence for how to think about designing a column: normalise first, then measure over time or across peers. The grammar enforces it. Raw metrics can be selected, classified, ranked and reduced, but only a percentage can be carried into a second operator — and a percentage is the only form that means the same thing on every coin in the pool.

The productive region is therefore narrower and sharper than 2,200 suggests: spread and distance, pointed at a well-chosen reference, then pushed through trajectory, aggregate, efficiency or rank. That is 166 shapes, and it is where nearly every numeric indicator worth building lives.

But do not stop at the column. The relationships a strategy actually trades — this coin against a benchmark, two of three filters agreeing, a regime holding while momentum turns — are expressed one layer up. The column layer supplies the quantities; sections and conditions turn them into a read.
